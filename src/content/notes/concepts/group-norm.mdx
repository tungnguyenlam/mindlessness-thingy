BatchNorm normalizes each channel across the mini-batch, which breaks when high-resolution images or video volumes force only one or two examples onto each GPU. [[layer-norm|LayerNorm]] avoids the batch axis by pooling all channels of one example together, but convolutional filters learn features of very different scales. GroupNorm takes the middle path: it divides an example's channels into $G$ groups and normalizes each group on its own <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="§3" />.

$$
\hat{x}_i = \frac{x_i - \mu_g}{\sqrt{\sigma_g^2 + \epsilon}}, \qquad y_i = \gamma_c \hat{x}_i + \beta_c
$$

<Words>
A 2D convolutional layer produces a four-dimensional activation tensor $x$ of shape $(N, C, H, W)$, where $N$ is the batch size, $C$ is the number of channels, and $H \times W$ is the spatial grid. An index $i = (n, c, h, w)$ picks one number in that tensor.

GroupNorm splits the $C$ channels of each example $n$ into $G$ contiguous groups of $C/G$ channels each <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="§3.1" />. Channel $c$ belongs to group $g = \lfloor c / (C/G) \rfloor$. For each example $n$ and group $g$, GroupNorm averages over all $m = (C/G) \cdot H \cdot W$ values in the group to find the group mean $\mu_g$ and variance $\sigma_g^2$, then subtracts $\mu_g$ and divides by $\sqrt{\sigma_g^2 + \epsilon}$, where $\epsilon$ is a small constant such as $10^{-5}$.

The learned scale $\gamma$ and shift $\beta$ are indexed by channel $c$, not by group $g$ <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="§3.1, Fig. 3" />. Even though $C/G$ channels share one $\mu_g$ and $\sigma_g$, each channel keeps its own $\gamma_c$ and $\beta_c$, giving $2C$ trainable parameters per layer. Setting $G = 1$ pools all $C$ channels together and recovers [[layer-norm|LayerNorm]], whereas setting $G = C$ normalizes each channel alone and recovers InstanceNorm <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="§3.1" />.
</Words>

<Words>
BatchNorm runs different math during training and evaluation <Cite id="batchnorm-paper" where="§3.1" />. During training, it normalizes each channel with the mean and variance of the current mini-batch over $N \cdot H \cdot W$ values while updating exponential moving averages. At evaluation, it switches to those frozen running averages.

When GPU memory limits the batch to $N = 1$ or $N = 2$ images per worker, BatchNorm's mini-batch estimates become noisy and drift from the running averages. On ResNet-50, shrinking from $32$ to $2$ images per GPU pushes BatchNorm's ImageNet error from $23.6\%$ to $34.7\%$ <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="Table 2" />. Fine-tuning on object detection also breaks unfrozen BatchNorm, forcing models to freeze its statistics or pay cross-GPU sync cost with SyncBN <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="§1, §2" />.

GroupNorm avoids both issues because the batch axis $N$ never enters $\mu_g$ or $\sigma_g$ <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="§3.1" />. It stores no running buffers, needs no cross-GPU communication, and runs the exact same deterministic formula during training, fine-tuning, and single-image inference. Across batch sizes from $32$ down to $2$, ResNet-50 with GroupNorm ($G = 32$) stays between $24.0\%$ and $24.2\%$ validation error <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="Table 2" />.
</Words>

<Words>
Why does dividing channels into groups beat both $G = 1$ ([[layer-norm|LayerNorm]]) and $G = C$ (InstanceNorm) on convolutional networks? At $G = 1$, all $C$ channels share one mean and variance, so high-energy filters dominate the variance estimate and squash quieter feature detectors, raising ResNet-50 error to $25.3\%$ <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="Table 3" />. At $G = C$, normalizing each channel over $(H, W)$ alone forces every channel's spatial mean to zero and erases relative firing strength across channels, raising error to $28.4\%$ <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="Table 3" />.

Any intermediate group count ($G \in \{8, 16, 32\}$ or fixing $C/G = 16$ channels per group) pools enough values $m = (C/G)HW$ for a steady estimate while letting separate groups keep distinct activation scales <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="§4.1, Table 3" />.

The main trade-off of plain GroupNorm appears at large batch sizes. At $N = 32$ per GPU, GroupNorm reaches *lower* training error than BatchNorm on ResNet-50, yet trails BatchNorm by $0.5\%$ on validation error ($24.1\%$ vs. $23.6\%$) <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="§4.1, Fig. 4" />. Because BatchNorm computes statistics over random mini-batches, its sampling noise acts as an implicit regularizer that deterministic GroupNorm lacks.
</Words>

<Words>
**Object detection, segmentation, and video.** Mask R-CNN and Faster R-CNN train with 1 or 2 high-resolution images per GPU. Replacing frozen BatchNorm with GroupNorm ($G = 32$) in the ResNet-50 FPN backbone and box head lifts COCO box AP from $38.6$ to $40.3$ ($40.8$ on a longer schedule) and enables training detectors from scratch on COCO at $39.5$ box AP versus $34.5$ for SyncBN <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="§4.2, Tables 6–7" />.

**Diffusion U-Nets and autoencoders.** Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPM) use GroupNorm ($G = 32$) in every U-Net residual and attention block <Cite id="ddpm-paper" where="§4, App. B" />. In a diffusion batch, each example sits at a different noise timestep $t$, so BatchNorm would average across incompatible noise levels. Diffusion U-Nets instead normalize each example with GroupNorm and modulate channels with timestep-projected scale and shift, the convolutional counterpart to [[adaln]] in [[dit]].

**Large-scale transfer learning.** Big Transfer (BiT) replaces BatchNorm with GroupNorm plus Weight Standardization across ResNet-V2 backbones up to ResNet-152$\times$4 so pre-training and small-batch downstream transfer never suffer from running-average drift <Cite id="bit-paper" where="§2.1, §3.3" />.
</Words>

<Words>
**InstanceNorm** ($G = C$) normalizes each channel over its spatial grid $(H, W)$ alone <Cite id="instancenorm-paper" where="§1" />. Ulyanov et al. showed that in style transfer, an image's global contrast and lighting live in its per-channel spatial mean and variance. Stripping those statistics per channel lets a stylization network paint a new texture cleanly, whereas in classification it discards useful contrast between channels <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="§4.1, Table 1" />.

**WeightNorm** <Cite id="weightnorm-paper" where="§2" /> and **Weight Standardization (WS)** <Cite id="weight-standardization-paper" where="§3" /> normalize the network's *weights* $W$ rather than its activations $x$. WeightNorm decouples each filter's length $g$ from its direction $v / \|v\|_2$, which avoids batch statistics but does not keep activation variance from growing with depth ($28.2\%$ error on ResNet-50 <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="Table 1, fn. 3" />).

Weight Standardization centers and scales each convolutional output filter $W_i$ to zero mean and unit variance across its fan-in $(C_{\text{in}}, K_h, K_w)$:

$$
\hat{W}_{i,j} = \frac{W_{i,j} - \mu_{W_i}}{\sqrt{\sigma_{W_i}^2 + \epsilon}}
$$

Qiao et al. showed that Weight Standardization smooths the loss landscape while GroupNorm steadies activation scales, just as [[layer-norm|LayerNorm]] and [[adaln]] do in [[transformer-block|Transformers]]. Combining **GroupNorm + Weight Standardization** closes the $0.5\%$ large-batch gap with BatchNorm on ImageNet and matches or beats large-batch BatchNorm even at $N = 1$ image per GPU <Cite id="weight-standardization-paper" where="§4–§5" />.
</Words>

<Callout type="pitfall">
Never swap GroupNorm into a BatchNorm-trained model without fine-tuning, and check that every layer's channel count $C$ is divisible by $G$. In narrow stem layers where $C < 32$, either pick a smaller $G$ (such as $G = 8$ or $G = \min(32, C)$) or fix the channels per group ($C/G = 16$) <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="Table 3" />. Also set `bias=False` on any convolution immediately before GroupNorm, because subtracting $\mu_g$ cancels any constant channel offset.
</Callout>

<Callout type="note">
Two common claims in normalization papers are contested. First, early papers attributed BatchNorm's speedups to reducing "internal covariate shift" <Cite id="batchnorm-paper" where="§1" />, but Santurkar et al. showed that normalization instead helps by making the loss surface and gradients smoother <Cite id="santurkar-bn-paper" where="§2–§4" />. Second, Wu and He motivate channel groups by analogy to SIFT/HOG orientation bins <Cite id="groupnorm-paper" where="§1, §3" />, yet standard SGD places no semantic constraint on adjacent channel indices, so the verified benefit is statistical: an intermediate pool size $m = (C/G)HW$ avoids both all-channel squashing ($G=1$) and per-channel contrast erasure ($G=C$).
</Callout>
