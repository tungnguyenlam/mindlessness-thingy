A diffusion or flow model needs a network that maps a noisy sample and its noise level back to the clean signal. In vision that job went to a convolutional U-Net by default <Cite id="dit-paper" where="§1" />. DiT swaps it for a plain [[transformer-block]] over image patches: the latent becomes a sequence, the noise level and class label enter every block through [[adaln]], and a linear head turns the tokens back into a prediction.

## Patches in, prediction out

The input is a latent $z$ of shape $I \times I \times C$ — in the paper, a frozen VAE's compressed image. Patchify cuts it into $p \times p$ patches and embeds each with one linear layer, giving

$$
T = (I / p)^2
$$

tokens of width $d$, followed by a sine-cosine positional embedding <Cite id="dit-paper" where="§3.2, Fig. 4" />. Each block mixes them with self-attention and a feed-forward network, with [[transformer-block]]'s usual residuals, while [[adaln]] sets the scale and shift from the timestep and label. The final norm and a linear layer decode every token back into a patch, and the tokens are rearranged into the spatial grid <Cite id="dit-paper" where="§3.2" />. In the paper's diffusion setup the head predicts noise and covariance, $2C$ channels per patch.

## What changes from a U-Net

An image U-Net is convolutional, mostly ResNet blocks, with spatial self-attention only at the lowest resolutions <Cite id="dit-paper" where="§1" />. DiT drops the down- and upsampling and keeps one width for every token. The paper's reading: the U-Net's inductive bias was not the essential part — forward-pass compute is what predicts quality, more Gflops for lower FID <Cite id="dit-paper" where="§5, Fig. 8" />.

## What it costs

Patch size is the compute dial. Halving $p$ quadruples $T$ and at least quadruples the transformer's Gflops, while parameter counts barely move <Cite id="dit-paper" where="§3.2, Fig. 4" />. Conditioning is the other dial: in-context tokens add almost nothing, adaLN adds almost nothing, and cross-attention adds about 15% <Cite id="dit-paper" where="§3.2" /> — yet adaLN-Zero wins on quality anyway <Cite id="dit-paper" where="§5, Fig. 5" />.

A DiT predicts one signal from one noisy input. What turns that prediction into a generator is the training objective and the sampler around it, which is where [[flow-matching]] comes in.
