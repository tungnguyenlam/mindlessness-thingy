The [[kv-cache]] grows with the number of key and value heads, and for [[mha]] there is one pair per query head. Multi-query attention (MQA) keeps all $n_h$ query heads and gives them one key head and one value head to share <Cite id="mqa-paper" where="§3" />. What each decoding step has to reload shrinks by a factor of $n_h$.

## How much it saves

Multi-head attention adds $2 n_h d_h$ numbers to the cache per token per layer. MQA adds $2 d_h$: the head dimension survives, the head count does not. The paper's bandwidth analysis says the same thing — the term that reloads keys and values each step shrinks by a factor of $n_h$ <Cite id="mqa-paper" where="§3.1" />.

MQA's cache is exactly $n_h$ times smaller than MHA's: move the head count and the ratio moves with it, whatever the other settings.

## What it costs

Shazeer measured the trade on WMT14 English-German, comparing an 8-head baseline against the same model with every attention layer converted <Cite id="mqa-paper" where="§4.1" />. Decoding got much faster; quality moved very little.

The step times come from incremental decoding of 128-token sequences in a batch of 1024 <Cite id="mqa-paper" where="§4.3" />; the ratio is the point, since the absolute numbers move with hardware. Quality dipped slightly, and stayed closer to the baseline than any variant that cut the head count or the head width instead <Cite id="mqa-paper" where="§4.2" />.

The saving is bandwidth, not arithmetic: every query head still attends over every past token <Cite id="mqa-paper" where="§3.1" />. [[gqa]] sits in between, giving each group of heads its own key and value.
