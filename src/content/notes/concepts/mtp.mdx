Next-token prediction gives each position one learning signal: predict the token that follows. Multi-token prediction (MTP) asks every position for the next $n$ tokens at once, through several heads on a shared trunk — a denser signal during training, and extra heads that can later draft for faster inference <Cite id="mtp-paper" where="Abstract" />.

The heads run in parallel on the same hidden state, each aiming one step further than the last, and their losses are summed <Cite id="mtp-paper" where="§2" />.

## The objective

In plain words: from position $t$, ask not only what comes next, but what comes after that, up to $n$ steps ahead, and grade every answer. The loss is the sum of the per-position cross-entropies <Cite id="mtp-paper" where="§2, eq. 2" />:

$$
L_n = -\sum_t \sum_{i=1}^{n} \log P_\theta(x_{t+i} \mid x_{t:1})
$$

To keep it tractable, the model splits into a shared trunk and $n$ independent heads, each predicting one future token through the shared unembedding matrix <Cite id="mtp-paper" where="§2" />. The first head is the usual next-token predictor; training simply stops ignoring the rest.

## Why it helps

Each position now carries $n$ loss terms instead of one, and the extra terms fall on whatever comes later. The paper reads this as reweighting the loss toward consequential tokens: a hard choice also constrains its own consequences, so it appears in several targets and earns a weight of $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$, against $n$ for an easy token <Cite id="mtp-paper" where="§5.1" />.

The heads that learn to predict further are also heads you can run as a drafter. The paper decodes greedily with blockwise parallel decoding — no separate draft model needed — and reports the drafts are accurate enough to be worth verifying <Cite id="mtp-paper" where="§2, Inference" />.

## What it costs

The heads are extra parameters, so a fair comparison has to take them from somewhere: the paper removes one trunk layer for every head layer it adds <Cite id="mtp-paper" where="§3" />. Logits are the memory bottleneck — $n$ heads would materialise $n$ logit blocks at once — so the implementation runs each head's forward and backward in turn and keeps only the trunk gradient <Cite id="mtp-paper" where="§2" />.

At inference the extra heads are dead weight unless you use them for drafting. And the gains depend on the task and on $n$: on natural language, four-target training slightly regressed multiple-choice benchmarks while improving summarisation <Cite id="mtp-paper" where="§3.7" />.

## In practice

DeepSeek-V3 trains a single MTP module on top of the main model, with its loss weighted 0.3 for the first 10T tokens and 0.1 afterwards <Cite id="deepseek-v3-report" where="§4.2" />. At inference the module drafts: the second-token prediction is accepted 85–90% of the time, giving 1.8× higher decoding throughput <Cite id="deepseek-v3-report" where="§5.4.3" />.
