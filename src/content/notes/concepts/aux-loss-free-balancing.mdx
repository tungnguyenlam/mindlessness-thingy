A [[moe]] router left to itself converges to sending most tokens to the same few favourite experts: those experts train faster and get picked even more. The usual fix is an auxiliary loss that penalises imbalance, but it adds gradients that pull against the language-modeling objective — a strong coefficient holds the load flat and costs quality, a weak one lets routing collapse <Cite id="loss-free-balancing-paper" where="§2.2" />. This design balances the load without adding anything to the loss.

Selection and weighting use two different scores, and that split is the whole idea: the bias can steer who runs without changing what the layer outputs.

## How it works

The biased score decides which experts run. The plain affinity decides how much each one counts:

$$
g_{i,t} =
\begin{cases}
s_{i,t} & \text{if } s_{i,t} + b_i \text{ is among the top } K_r \text{ of } \{ s_{j,t} + b_j \} \\
0 & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

$s_{i,t}$ is the affinity between token $t$ and expert $i$, $b_i$ is expert $i$'s stored bias, and $g_{i,t}$ is the weight that multiplies the expert's output <Cite id="loss-free-balancing-paper" where="§3, Eq. (3)" />. The bias never enters $g_{i,t}$, so it steers selection and no gradient flows through it <Cite id="loss-free-balancing-paper" where="§3" />.

## Updating the bias

After each training step the biases move one fixed step $\gamma$ towards balance: down for experts that got more tokens than their share, up for the ones that got less <Cite id="loss-free-balancing-paper" where="§3, Algorithm 1" />.

$$
b_i \leftarrow b_i + \gamma \cdot \mathrm{sign}(\bar{c} - c_i)
$$

$c_i$ is the number of tokens expert $i$ received and $\bar{c}$ the average across experts. The count comes from the previous batch, not the current sequence, so no information about future tokens leaks into earlier ones <Cite id="loss-free-balancing-paper" where="§3, §5.2" />. The paper writes the step as $u$; DeepSeek-V3's report calls it $\gamma$ <Cite id="deepseek-v3-report" where="§4.2" />.

<Stepper title="One training step of the bias loop">
  <Step title="Score and select">
    Compute the affinity of every token to every routed expert, add the stored bias, and pick the top $K_r$ experts. The gates that scale the outputs come from the affinity alone.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Train on the usual loss">
    Forward and backward pass as normal. The model updates from the language-modeling objective only; the bias is not a parameter and receives no gradient.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Count the load">
    After the step, count how many tokens each expert received across the batch and take the average across experts.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Nudge each bias">
    Overloaded experts get $b_i - \gamma$, underloaded experts get $b_i + \gamma$. The next batch is routed with the updated biases, and the loop repeats.
  </Step>
</Stepper>

## Does the load actually hold

The paper trains 1B- and 3B-parameter models and reports validation perplexity next to MaxVio, the most overloaded expert's excess above a perfectly balanced load <Cite id="loss-free-balancing-paper" where="§4.1, Table 2" />. Balance improves by an order of magnitude while perplexity improves slightly, which is the trade the auxiliary loss could not escape.

## What it costs

The gain replaces a loss coefficient with a step size that has to be tuned. Too small and the biases converge slowly, so the load stays uneven early in training; too large and the biases fluctuate late in training and balance degrades again. The paper's sweep settles on $10^{-3}$ between $10^{-4}$ and $10^{-2}$ <Cite id="loss-free-balancing-paper" where="§4.3" />. DeepSeek-V3 also runs the step down to zero at the end, freezing the routing, and still keeps a small loss:

<Callout type="pitfall">
Pinning the load exactly instead — every expert gets the same number of tokens — means an earlier token's experts depend on later tokens in the sequence. The paper calls that future-token leakage and estimates it at over 50 bits per token for a 9-layer, 16-expert, top-2 model <Cite id="loss-free-balancing-paper" where="§5.2, Appendix D.1" />. A bias on the scores keeps routing causal.
</Callout>
