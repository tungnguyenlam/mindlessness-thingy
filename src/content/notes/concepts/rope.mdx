Attention ([[mha]]) scores a query against a key with a dot product, and a dot product has no sense of order: shuffle the sentence and every score comes out the same. Position has to enter somewhere. Rotary position embedding (RoPE) enters it as a rotation: every query and key turns by an angle that its own position sets, so a pair's score depends only on how far apart the two tokens are <Cite id="roformer-paper" where="§3.2" />.

## The idea in one picture

<Stepper title="Two tokens, two angles, one score">
  <Step title="Two vectors, position unused">
    A query from the token at position $m$ and a key from the token at position $n$ come out of the [[mha]] projections as plain vectors $q$ and $k$. Scored as they are, $q^{\top} k$ says nothing about where either token sits.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Turn each by its own angle">
    Multiply the query by the rotation $R_m$ and the key by $R_n$. The angle comes from the position index; a rotation turns a vector without changing its length.
  </Step>
  <Step title="The angles compose">
    The score is now $q_m^{\top} k_n = q^{\top} R_m^{\top} R_n k$, and $(R_m)^{\top} R_n = R_{n-m}$: the two rotations add up to the single rotation for the gap between the tokens.
  </Step>
  <Step title="Slide the pair along">
    Push both tokens further down the sequence by the same amount. Both angles shift together, the composed rotation stays $R_{n-m}$, and the pair's score does not move.
  </Step>
</Stepper>

Position enters only as a rotation angle, so a pair's score depends on the gap $n - m$ and nothing else <Cite id="roformer-paper" where="§3.1" />.

Read each vector as a bundle of arrows, one per plane of numbers. Turning both arrows of a plane, each by its own angle, changes the angle between them by the difference of the two turns. That difference is all the score can see.

Rotations compose by adding angles, which is the whole trick:

$$
(R_m)^{\top} R_n = R_{n-m}
$$

so the score collapses to one rotation for the gap:

$$
q_m^{\top} k_n = (R_m q)^{\top} (R_n k) = q^{\top} R_m^{\top} R_n k = q^{\top} R_{n-m} k
$$

Only $n - m$ survives: the same distance scores the same anywhere in the sequence.

$R_m$ is block-diagonal. The $d_h$ numbers of a head are read as planes, and plane $i$ turns at its own frequency $\theta_i = 10000^{-2(i-1)/d_h}$ (the paper writes the rotated width as $d$) <Cite id="roformer-paper" where="§3.2.2" />. Over a long gap a fast plane turns far and its contribution fades, while a slow plane has barely turned. The paper proves that decay of the score with distance <Cite id="roformer-paper" where="§3.3" />.

Nothing per position is learned: the angle comes from the index and the fixed frequencies. The paper lists sequence-length flexibility among RoPE's properties <Cite id="roformer-paper" where="Abstract" />.

## What it costs

The rotation itself is cheap: the paper gives an efficient realization that avoids multiplying by a full matrix <Cite id="roformer-paper" where="§3.4.2" />. The real cost sits in the [[kv-cache]]: a key is stored already rotated, so it belongs to its position and no other. [[mla]] pays for that coupling directly, since a key rebuilt from a latent cannot be rotated after the fact, and carries position in a separate channel instead.
