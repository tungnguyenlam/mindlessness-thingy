Hand-pose estimators rarely output a triangle mesh directly. They output a small vector of numbers, and a fixed model turns that vector into a mesh — the way [[stablehand]] predicts a hand's parameters rather than its geometry. MANO is that model: pose and shape parameters in, a posed hand mesh out <Cite id="mano-paper" where="§3" />.

## How it works

MANO follows the SMPL recipe <Cite id="mano-paper" where="§3, Eqs. 1–2" />. A template mesh is displaced by two learned sets of vertex offsets — shape blend shapes $B_S(\beta)$, which vary identity, and pose blend shapes $B_P(\theta)$, which correct what plain skinning gets wrong — then posed and skinned:

$$
M(\beta, \theta) = W\Big(\bar{T} + B_S(\beta) + B_P(\theta),\; J(\beta),\; \theta, \mathcal{W}\Big)
$$

$\bar{T}$ is the template, $J(\beta)$ the joint locations, which a sparse linear regressor predicts from the vertices, and $\mathcal{W}$ the skinning weights. Rotations are axis-angle, one triple per joint <Cite id="mano-paper" where="§3.3" />. The pose blend shapes matter: linear blend skinning collapses at the knuckles, and the learned corrections restore the bulge of bent fingers <Cite id="mano-paper" where="§3, Fig. 9; §5.1" />.

Downstream systems regress the two vectors themselves. The pose vector $\theta$ holds three axis-angle numbers per joint — 45 in total for the 15 finger joints, plus the global orientation — while $\beta$ holds the shape coefficients <Cite id="mano-paper" where="§3.3" />. The paper never states how many shape coefficients the released model keeps or how many vertices the template has; both are left unverified here.

## Where it stops

The pose space is linear and low-dimensional, which is the point and also the limit. The paper's own failure cases are poses that fall outside that space, and hands holding objects: contact and object interaction are not part of the model <Cite id="mano-paper" where="§5.2, Fig. 16" />. What comes out is a parametric mesh — parameters in, a posed surface and joints out.

The payoff is the reason it became standard output: a fixed, differentiable, animation-ready mesh with joints, from a few dozen numbers <Cite id="mano-paper" where="§6" />.

<Callout type="pitfall">
Predicting MANO parameters and predicting a mesh are not interchangeable tasks. A system trained to output $\theta$ can only produce meshes MANO can represent — a clenched fist around an object, or a pose outside the PCA space, will be flattened into the nearest expressible hand no matter how good the estimator is.
</Callout>
